Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

customer_order · requester: Tauhid Zaman · CC-TEE-WHITE, size S, qty 1 · invoice_id 501 · status open
"Needs a tee in size S."

Expected

Who the Boss calls first, and why

Inventory, first. This is a customer order, and the only question that matters at the start is whether the shop can simply hand over the tee. If size S is on the shelf, the ticket is a two-step job and nobody else needs to be involved. The Boss should not call Accounting first even though the ticket carries an invoice_id, because until the stock check comes back short, there is no reason to spend money at all.

Delegations I expect

  1. Boss → Inventory: can we fill one CC-TEE-WHITE in size S, and if not, who restocks it and how long do they take? I expect this to come back with a shortfall of 1 and with Bulldog Print Co (vendor 1, 5-day lead) named as the only apparel source — and blocked, because that vendor has an open unpaid invoice.
  2. Inventory → Accounting (peer-to-peer, not routed back through the Boss): what is the story on invoice 501, and can we clear it? This is the delegation I most want to see, because it is the one that proves the agents follow the dependency rather than each reporting a dead end. Accounting should find invoice 501 is $840.00, 3 days overdue, owed to vendor 1 — the same vendor — and should raise a pending approval rather than paying.
  3. Boss → Customer Service: draft Tauhid a note explaining the tee is out of stock in S, that a reprint is being arranged, and what the realistic timing is. The draft must not promise a delivery date as if it were confirmed, because the reprint depends on a payment a human has not approved yet.

I do not expect Facilities to be touched. The shop space has nothing to do with a tee order, and calling it would be wasted tokens.

"Student org wants 20 hoodies, size M, asking for a bulk discount."

Expected

Who the Boss calls first, and why

Inventory, first — and this is the ticket where that ordering is easiest to get wrong. The request sounds like a pricing question, so the tempting move is to call Accounting and negotiate a discount. Delegations I expect

I do not expect Facilities to be involved.

Reflection

Written after the full run of 2026-10-09. Evidence is on the ticket tabs and the Cash tab of this page, and in output/audit_trail.json (900 recorded steps).

1. How did the agents perform on each ticket?

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee: strong diagnosis, weak follow-through

This was the team's best reasoning and its worst efficiency. Inventory did the thing I most wanted to see: it did not just report "out of stock", it checked get_vendor_status, found Bulldog Print Co blocked by their own unpaid invoice 501, and named the $840.00 as the obstacle. Accounting independently reached the same invoice and wrote the reason into approval 1 in plain language. The shop's real problem — that a customer could not get a tee because of an unpaid bill — was correctly identified on the first pass, with no invented numbers.

The weakness was that it took seven passes. Each run advanced the ticket only to the next human decision and then stopped, and twice the agents stalled on a problem that was mine rather than theirs: after invoice 501 was paid, Accounting looked for it among open invoices, could not find it, and reported a "data discrepancy". That is a reasonable thing to say when your only tool lists open invoices and the row has vanished from it. I had not given them a way to ask about one invoice by id. Grade: A on judgement, C on autonomy.

Ticket 102 — Rent: clean, and right for the right reason

Three passes, two delegations each, no wasted calls. Facilities did exactly what I hoped: it treated the email as a claim and checked it against lease 1 before anybody reached for the cheque book, confirming $2,400.00 due 2026-09-02, two days out and not yet overdue. Accounting prepared it and refused to pay it. That is the whole control working as designed.

One genuine miss: Accounting checked that $2,552.00 covered $2,400.00 and stopped there. It never mentioned that ticket 101 had its own claims on the same account. Its prompt tells it to sequence competing obligations, but it only sees the ticket it is asked about, so "competing" is invisible to it. Grade: A−.

Ticket 103 — Hoodie discount: correct answer, wrong starting point

The Boss called Accounting first on all five passes, which is the specific mistake I predicted on this tab: it read "bulk discount" as a pricing question when the binding constraint was that only 8 of the 20 hoodies exist. The answer survived only because Accounting checked stock itself before pricing anything. Good outcome, lucky process — a less conscientious specialist would have quoted 20 units at 10% off and the shop would have promised something it could not deliver.

What the team got right was the money. Accounting priced from real margin ($22.00 cost against $58.00 list, break-even at 62.1% off) and recommended 10%, which keeps $27.40 a unit. That is a defensible commercial answer, not a number pulled out of the air. And when the $264.00 restock could not be funded, Customer Service wrote the Yale AI Club an honest partial offer rather than papering over it. Grade: B+.

Across all three: the one thing that worked best

Not an agent — the guardrails. A human approved a $264.00 purchase order and the pay tool refused it anyway, because $152.00 would not cover it. Every payment in the Cash tab carries the name of the person who approved it, no agent ever moved money, and no message ever reached a customer. The things I put in code held; the things I put only in prompts (call Inventory first, sequence competing obligations) were followed inconsistently.

The biggest thing I got wrong was the shape of the team. I wrote the plan expecting peer-to-peer chains — Inventory asking Accounting, Facilities handing to Accounting. I built full connectivity so that could happen: every agent carries an ask_ tool for all four others. Across 15 runs and 900 audit steps, exactly one sideways delegation occurred (Customer Service asking Inventory about stock). Everything else was the Boss fanning out and stitching the answers together. Full connectivity was available and almost entirely unused, because each specialist receives a narrow question it can usually answer alone. The expensive architecture I was proudest of is the part the run did not need.

The second thing I got wrong was treating a ticket as one run. My plan read like a single pass per ticket. In practice a ticket is a conversation with a human in the middle: prepare, stop, wait for approval, resume. 101 took seven passes, 103 five. Any prediction that ignores the human's turn is going to be wrong about effort by a factor of five.

The third was my own tooling. Two tools had to be added mid-run — get_invoice (ask about one invoice by id, so a paid bill is not mistaken for a missing record) and draft_purchase_order (actually order the restock, instead of only recommending one). Without the second, ticket 101 could never have closed: the agents could describe the fix forever but had no way to do it. The tools I shipped let the team diagnose; they did not let it act.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

Ticket 102, beyond any argument. The entire job is: read lease_id from the ticket, call get_lease, compare the amount to the notice, check the balance, raise an approval. That is four tool calls in a fixed order with no judgement in between. Routing it through a Boss, a Facilities agent and an Accounting agent cost 35,206 tokens and three model round-trips per pass to produce what a single agent would have produced in one. The specialists added nothing a plain function could not: the lease either matches the notice or it does not.

Ticket 101 after the first pass is the same story. Once invoice 501 was paid, every later pass was just re-reading state — is the invoice paid, can the vendor ship, is the order placed — and the Boss still delegated to all three specialists every single time, seven times over, at roughly 50–79k tokens a run. A single agent holding all the tools would have answered "yes, yes, yes, close it" in one call.

Where the multi-agent split did earn its cost is 103, and only in one specific way: it forced the stock question and the price question to be answered by different reasoners. Accounting proposing 10% knew nothing about the 12-unit shortfall when it started; Inventory's answer had to exist separately for the two to be reconciled. In a single agent those two concerns blur, and the failure mode — quoting a confident price for stock you do not have — is exactly the one that reaches a customer. That is a real argument for separation, but it is an argument for two reasoners, not five.

My honest conclusion: for this shop, one agent with these twenty-odd MCP tools plus a hard human-approval gate would have resolved all three tickets faster, more cheaply, and with fewer stalls. The value I actually got from five agents was not better answers — it was a readable board. The lanes on the dashboard let a human see who claimed what, and the audit trail attributes each tool call to a role. That is worth something for trust and review. It is not worth 5× the tokens for a three-ticket shop.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools I built

  1. "The Crest Mug is at zero and someone wants 30 for a reunion." CC-MUG-CREST is sitting at qty 0 in Aisle D right now, exactly like the tee was. The existing path handles it unchanged: check_stock_for_order gives the shortfall, list_vendors finds Elm City Gifts (mugs and small goods, 3-day lead), get_vendor_status confirms they have no open invoice so they can ship — unlike Bulldog Print Co — and draft_purchase_order prices 30 units at $3.50 into a $105.00 approval. Customer Service drafts the reunion organiser a date. No new tools at all.
  2. "Can we afford October rent and the hoodie restock?" After this run the shop is down to $152.00 with rent of $2,400.00 due 2026-10-02 and a cancelled $264.00 purchase order still unfilled. Facilities has list_leases with days-until-due, Accounting has get_cash_balance, list_invoices and list_approvals, and the Boss can sequence them. The team can already say "you are $2,512.00 short for October and the rent has to win" — which is the most useful sentence anyone could say to this shop today.
  3. "A second student org wants 15 hoodies in L." There are 14 in L (list_stock_for_sku shows 4 S / 8 M / 14 L / 6 XL), so this is a shortfall of one against a size that is actually well stocked. quote_discount prices the offer and proves it stays profitable, Inventory confirms the 14, and Customer Service drafts an offer of 14 now and 1 to follow. Every tool needed already exists and has been exercised on ticket 103.

5. Three problems this team could not solve, and what I would need

  1. "The reprint arrived on the fifth — is the order actually done?" Purchase order 1 is marked ordered with an expected arrival of 2026-09-05, and nothing in my system can ever receive it. There is no tool that increments inventory.qty when goods turn up, so the tee will sit at qty 0 forever while a purchase order claims it is coming. Ticket 101 is marked resolved on the strength of an order, not a delivery. I would need a receive_purchase_order(purchase_order_id, qty_received) tool writing to inventory and closing the order, a list_overdue_purchase_orders check against the shop date, and a Receiving agent that runs on a schedule rather than on a ticket — because nobody files a ticket saying "the van did not come".
  2. "We sold eight hoodies to the AI Club — where is the money?" Ticket 103 ends with an agreed 10% discount on 8 units, $417.60 of revenue, and the shop's balance does not move by a cent. Cash only goes out in this model: there is no sales table, no customer invoice, no receipt. So the team cannot answer "can we afford October rent" honestly, because it can only see outflows. I would need a sales table with record_sale(sku, size, qty, unit_price, ticket_id) that both decrements inventory and credits cash, an issue_customer_invoice tool, and a Revenue agent chasing unpaid customer invoices the way Accounting chases vendor ones. Without this the shop's agents watch it slowly bleed to zero and report it as normal.
  3. "Elm City Properties is raising rent 12% and wants a new 2-year lease." Facilities can read lease 1 and that is the limit of it. It cannot model what a 12% rise does to a shop with $152.00 in the bank, cannot compare it against another space, and cannot negotiate — and this is the decision that would actually close Campus Customs. I would need a forecast_cash(months) tool projecting leases, invoices and recurring costs forward from desk.date_today, a model_lease_scenario tool for comparing terms, a market_rents reference table so a comparison is grounded in data rather than opinion, and a Planning agent whose job is multi-month trade-offs. Every agent I built answers "what is true today"; none can answer "what happens in six months", which is the only question that matters for a lease.

The honest summary

All three tickets resolved, $3,248.00 moved with a human's name on every payment, one unaffordable purchase refused by code rather than by judgement, and not one message sent to a customer. The agents were good at reading the shop and bad at finishing without being asked again. The failures were mostly mine — missing tools, a prompt that contradicted itself about when a ticket is done, and a status vocabulary with no word for "ordered but not yet arrived", which made the Boss call a finished ticket "blocked" four times running. The agents were about as good as the tools I handed them, which is the actual lesson.
